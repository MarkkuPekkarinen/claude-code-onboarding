# Opportunity Cost Analysis

Every commitment consumes resources — time, money, energy, attention. Every yes is simultaneously a no to everything else those resources could do. This framework makes the invisible tradeoffs visible before a commitment is made.

## Core Principle

Opportunity cost is not about what you are giving up in theory. It is about the specific highest-value alternative use of the same resources. Most people evaluate commitments in isolation: "Is this worth doing?" The right question is: "Is this worth doing *more than anything else I could do with the same time, money, and energy?*"

## When to Use

- Evaluating whether to take on a new project, role, partnership, or investment
- Deciding between two or more competing uses of limited resources
- When a commitment "seems worth it" but something feels off
- When you are about to say yes to something without having asked "compared to what?"

**Not for:** Decisions where one option is strictly dominant across all dimensions. If Option A is better in every way, this framework adds no value — just take Option A.

## The Three Resources

Always analyze across all three. People often focus only on money and ignore time and attention.

| Resource | Why It Matters | Common Mistake |
|----------|---------------|----------------|
| **Time** | Finite and non-renewable; the real constraint for most decisions | Underestimating how much a commitment takes once it is running |
| **Money** | Finite in the short term; compounding in the long term | Evaluating the cost without estimating the alternative return |
| **Attention** | Most limited of the three; quality of thinking degrades with split focus | Treating "I'll still have time for X" as the same as "X will still get my best thinking" |

## Interactive Method

### Step 1: Specify the Commitment

Ask the user to describe the commitment in concrete terms:
- *"What specifically are you committing to? What does it require over the next 3 months, 6 months, 12 months?"*
- *"How much time per week? How much money? What decisions will it require your attention on?"*

If the user cannot answer these questions, the commitment is underspecified and they should not commit yet.

### Step 2: Make the Tradeoffs Explicit — What You Are Saying No To

Ask: *"If you take this on, what specifically are you no longer able to do, or no longer able to do as well?"*

Probe across all three resources:

- **Time:** *"What currently gets that time? What would be delayed, reduced, or dropped?"*
- **Money:** *"What else could that capital do? What investments, hires, or experiments are you not making?"*
- **Attention:** *"What suffers when this is on your mind? What is the most important thing that will get less of your focus?"*

Push for specific names, not categories. Not "other projects" — which project? Not "personal time" — which relationship, which health habit, which skill?

### Step 3: Identify the Highest-Value Alternative

Ask: *"Of everything you would be giving up, what is the single highest-value alternative use of the same resources?"*

This is the true opportunity cost. It is not the average of all alternatives — it is the best one.

Then ask: *"If you were comparing this commitment directly against [highest-value alternative], and had to pick one, which builds more long-term value?"*

Do not let the user compare against a vague "other things." Make it a specific head-to-head.

### Step 4: Long-Term Value Assessment

Evaluate both options (the commitment and the best alternative) on long-term value:

| Dimension | Commitment | Best Alternative |
|-----------|-----------|-----------------|
| **Compounding returns** | Does this get better over time, or is it a fixed return? | Same question |
| **Optionality** | Does this open future options or close them? | Same question |
| **Reversibility** | Can you exit without major cost if it proves wrong? | Same question |
| **Alignment** | Does this move toward where you are trying to go in 3 years? | Same question |

### Step 5: The Reframe

This step is mandatory. Ask: *"Instead of 'Should I do this?', answer this: 'Is this worth doing more than [specific best alternative]?'"*

The reframe from "yes/no" to "this versus that" is the purpose of the framework. A decision that looks good in isolation often looks different when compared directly to a specific alternative.

### Step 6: Hidden Costs

Before concluding, check for hidden costs that make the real opportunity cost larger than it appears:

- **Ramp-up cost:** The time to get up to speed before the commitment produces value
- **Context-switching cost:** The cost in quality and time of switching attention between this and other commitments
- **Exit cost:** What it costs to stop once started — social, financial, reputational
- **Option foreclosure:** Commitments that prevent taking other opportunities that may appear later

## Common Mistakes

| Mistake | What to Do Instead |
|---------|-------------------|
| Comparing against a vague "other things" | Name the specific best alternative and compare directly |
| Only evaluating money, ignoring time and attention | Analyze all three resources explicitly |
| Assuming current allocation is the right baseline | The status quo is also a choice with opportunity costs |
| Treating "I can still do both" as settled | Ask specifically which one gets the best version of the user's time and thinking |
| Deciding based on sunk cost ("I've already invested X") | Past investment is irrelevant; only future costs and benefits matter |
